Abstract 24 

The noradrenergic locus coeruleus and its neuromodulatory cortical projections are 25 

critical for adaptive behavior, however how they contribute to human implicit learning in 26 

novel environments is not yet well understood, due to the challenges of assessing this system 27 

non -invasively . Here, we combined multimodal neuroimaging —including locus -coeruleus -28 

sensitive structural MRI, concurrent pupillometry –EEG, pupillometry –fMRI, and PET -29 

derived noradrenergic transporter maps —with repeated behavioral asse ssments to investigate 30 

noradrenergic contributions to implicit learning across younger and older adults (n = 77). 31 

Salient expectation -violating stimuli elicited pupil dilation, indicating enhanced 32 

neuromodulation, activated the action -mode network and deac tivated the default -mode 33 

network. Pupil -linked BOLD responses suggested a functional coupling between the locus 34 

coeruleus and action -mode network, further supported by spatial overlap of activation patterns 35 

with PET -derived noradrenergic transporter maps. Locus coeruleus MRI -guided functional 36 

connectivity analyses demonstrated coupled locus coeruleus and anterior insula activity ,37 

suggesting a noradrenergic role in shifting cortical dynamics toward action -oriented 38 

processing. Behaviorally, participants impli citly learned the statistical task structure over time, 39 

shifting their response times based on stimulus probabilities . Critically, stronger locus 40 

coeruleus integrity, greater task -related anterior insula activation, and more pronounced pupil 41 

dilation were associated with enhanced implicit learning, highlighting the behavioral 42 

relevance of noradrenergic neuromodulation. Notably, noradrenergic responses and their link 43 

to learning were preserved across age groups, suggesting a robust noradrenergic role in 44 

supp orting adaptive behavior throughout adulthood. Complementary electrophysiological 45 

analyses revealed pupil -linked increases in cortical excitability that were coupled with shifts 46 

from default -mode to action -mode network activation. Together, these findings provide novel 47 

insights into the neuromodulatory mechanisms underlying learning and cognitive flexibility, 48 

emphasizing the pivotal role of locus coeruleus –action -mode network interactions in 49 

behavioral adaptation. 50 

Keywords: locus coeruleus, neuromodulation, noradrenaline, norepinephrine, MRI, EEG, PET, 51 

pupillometry, attention, memory, implicit learning, aging 52

1. Introduction 53 

Optimal behavior requires dynamic adaptation to changing environments 1. On a 54 

neurochemical level, unexpected environmental changes tran siently increase noradrenaline 55 

release from the locus coeruleus, a brainstem nucleus that facilitates attention and learning 2–4.56 

Specifically, noradrenaline release has long been conceptualized as a neural interrupt signal 57 

that promotes rapid behavioral adaptation to new environmental imperatives 5–8. The 58 

momentary processing of salient stimuli is thereby facilitated by a noradrenergic enhancement 59 

of neural gain, effectively increasing signal -to -noise ratios in neural circuits 2,9 –12 . By contrast, 60 

long -term adjustments to salient events are mediated by noradrenergic influences on 61 

hippocampal synaptic plasticity 3,13 –16 .62 

In a similar vein, more recent accounts propose that the locus coeruleus is activated 63 

when predictions about the world are violated. Th e resulting noradrenaline release helps 64 

refine internal models of the world by adjusting the rate of learning in cortical target 65 

regions 17 –21 , but direct evidence from humans is scarce. 66 

To enable these functions, the locus coeruleus receives critical con textual information 67 

about stimulus salience and utility from cortical structures such as the anterior cingulate and 68 

insula 2,9 . In primates, these forebrain structures send major direct inputs to the locus coeruleus 69 

to recruit noradrenergic neuromodulatio n and modulate brain -wide processing according to 70 

environmental demands 9.71 

The anterior cingulate and insular cortices have recently been grouped into the action -72 

mode network 22 , overlapping with the cingulo -opercular network or ventral attention 73 

network 6,23,24 . The action -mode network is closely linked to initiating and maintaining states 74 

of heightened arousal and focused attention, as well as the generation and updating of actions 75 

based on salient internal or external signals 22,25 . In this context, arousal is defined as a 76 

continuum of sensitivity to environmental stimuli 26 , tightly regulated by noradrenergic 77 

neuromodulation 27 –29 . In line with this, it has been proposed that the locus coeruleus 78 

modulates or is part of the action -mode network 6,22,30 , but direct in vivo evidence remains 79 

limited. 80 

Aging dysregulates the noradrenergic system 31 –33 , possibly due to the age -related 81 

accumulation of pathology 34 –37 . In accordance with this, converging evidence based on 82 

cognitive assessm ents suggests that age differences in proxies of noradrenergic 83 

neuromodulation contribute to late -life impairments in attention 38 –40 , learning, and memory 41 –84  43 ; i.e., in situations when participants were explicitly instructed about the cognitive testin g. 85 

However, the role of the locus coeruleus in how younger and older adults implicitly learn to 86 

adapt their behavior in new situations remains underexplored. 87 

Here, we investigated the implications of locus coeruleus –cortical network 88 

interactions for impli cit behavioral adaptation using a multimodal age -comparative approach. 89 

To this end, we used a modified conditioned oddball task, previously shown to reliably 90 

increase locus coeruleus spiking in non -human primates 44 . In this variant of the oddball task, 91 

participants detect infrequent, distinctive stimuli amid a series of repetitive standard stimuli. 92 

To further increase recruitment of the locus coeruleus, infrequent oddball stimuli are pre -93 

conditioned by repeatedly pairing them with appetitive or aversive o utcomes 40,44 –46 . Our 94 

participants completed the conditioned oddball task twice on consecutive days, responding 95 

with different button presses depending on stimulus identity (see Figure 1). Trial -level 96 

behavioral responses from the task allowed us to exami ne implicit learning within and across 97 

sessions. In this context, implicit learning refers to how initially naïve participants pick up the 98 

statistical regularities of the originally new but increasingly familiar task environment and 99 

optimize their behavior over time (i.e., without explicit instructions). 100 

To overcome challenges in non -invasive assessment posed by the locus coeruleus’ 101 

small size and location in the brainstem 47 –49 , we relied on several recent additions to the 102

electrophysiological analyses revealed pupil -linked increases in cortical excitability that were 128

Together, these results suggest that noradrenergic signals support refining internal models of 130 

environmental contingencies for behavioral adaptation by modulating attention and memory 131 

following salient ev ents. By integrating advanced MRI, pupillometry, EEG, and functional 132 

connectivity analyses, we reveal critical insights into how neuromodulatory signals 133 

dynamically drive behavioral flexibility. 134

Noradrenergic transporter distribution overlaps with pupil -linked BOLD activation patterns 233 

If indeed the observed activation pattern is supported by noradrenergic release in the 234 

cortex, one would expect overlapping spatial distributions with the noradrenergic system. To 235 

test this, we leveraged public PET -derived maps of the cortical and subcortical distribution of 236 

the noradrenergic transporter 56,70 –74 . We then assessed the voxel -wise association of 237 

noradrenergic t ransporter expression and pupil -linked BOLD activation. We detected a robust 238 

correlation (Hesse et al. map: r = 0.189; ppermutation -corr < 0.001), that we replicated using an 239 

alternative noradrenergic transporter map (Ding et al. map: r = 0.161; ppermutation -corr < 0.001; 240 

Figure 5). This suggests that those parts of the brain that activated when pupils dilated 241 

overlapped with those housing noradrenergic receptors. 242 

To further contextualize these results, we computed two negative control analyses. 243 

Specifically, we linked two brain -wide GABA A receptor distribution maps 75,76 to our pupil -244 

linked BOLD maps, which we assumed to show no or even a negative association 77 . In line 245 

with this, we found that GABA A receptor expression showed a non -significant negative 246 

relation with the pupil –fMRI pattern (Nørgaard et al map: r = –0.02 8; ppermutation -corr = 0.063; 247 

Dukart et al. map: r = –0.045; ppermutation -corr = 0.056; Figure 5). 248 

Finally, given that large parts of the brainstem –midbrain, overlapping with 249 

noradrenergic, but also serotonergic and dopaminergic nuclei, activated when pu pils dilated, 250 

we ran two additional analyses to determine whether similar effects extended to other 251 

neuromodulatory systems. Individually, both serotonergic (Fazio et al. map: r = 0.246; 252 

ppermutation -corr < 0.001; 56 ) and dopaminergic (Sasaki et al. map: r = 0.241; ppermutation -corr <253 

0.001; 78 ) transporter expression showed a comparable spatial correspondence with the pupil –254 

fMRI activation pattern. While the cortical innervation of dopaminergic, serotonergic and 255 

noradrenergic nuclei is partially overlapp ing, there are also considerable differences between 256 

these neuromodulatory systems 3. To test if the expression pattern of noradrenergic 257 

transporters explains unique variance in the observed pupil -linked BOLD maps over and 258 

above the other neuromodulatory systems, we estimated multiple regression analyses. 259 

Specifically, we compared a base model (including serotonergic and dopaminergic transporter 260 

expression as predictors) to a full model additionally including a predictor for the 261 

noradrenergic transporter ( Table 1). Importantly, we found the full model substantially 262 

outperformed the base model, suggesting a unique association of noradrenergic transporter 263 

expression and pupil -linked BOLD activation ( Δχ²( df = 1) = 27373; ppermutation -corr < 0.001; for 264 

the expl ained variance of both models, see Table S4). In addition to voxel -wise spatial 265 

correlations (Figure 5, Table 1), we quantified overlap using Dice coefficients (Figure 6, 266 

Table 2), yielding comparable results. 267

3. Discussion 531 

This study investigated the role of the locus coeruleus and its cortical targets in how 532 

younger and older participants adapt their behavior in dynamic environments with novel 533 

observations. We took advantage of repeated assessm ents of an experimental task shown to 534 

modulate noradrenergic activity in primates 44 and a dedicated multimodal imaging protocol to 535 

identify neuromodulatory contributions to implicit learning. 536 

We found that salient expectation -violating stimuli dilated participants’ pupils, 537 

indicating heightened neuromodulatory activity, and triggered a shift from default -mode to 538 

action -mode network activation that was associated with increased cortical excitabi lity. 539 

Combining pupil and BOLD data, we observed that neuromodulatory brainstem –midbrain as 540 

well as action -mode network regions scaled their activity with pupil size. This suggests an 541 

interplay between the noradrenergic system and the action -mode network, which we 542 

confirmed using PET -derived transporter maps and locus coeruleus -centered functional 543 

connectivity analyses. Behavioral data showed that participants implicitly learned the 544 

statistical structure of the novel task environment, resulting in quicker r esponses for more 545 

prevalent trial categories at the expense of slower responses to rare oddballs. Linking brain 546 

and behavior, we found that this implicit learning pattern was more pronounced in participants 547 

with higher levels of markers associated with the noradrenergic system and its cortical targets. 548 

Taken together, this study suggests noradrenergic neuromodulation may upregulate the action -549 

mode network to facilitate (implicit) learning for behavioral adaptation. 550

Fear conditioned stimuli evoke robust neu romodulatory responses 552 

To increase recruitment of the locus coeruleus, a subset of our experimental stimuli 553 

was pre -conditioned before the main task by repeatedly pairing them with an aversive 554 

outcome 40,44 –46 . Conditioned stimuli (CS+) elicited greater p upil dilation than perceptually 555 

matched control stimuli (CS –), suggesting increased neuromodulatory drive. Moreover, these 556 

effects remained stable across age groups and assessment days, highlighting the robustness of 557 

the conditioned response. Our findings are in line with earlier work in rodents demonstrating 558 

increased locus coeruleus spiking 102 and noradrenergic axonal activation 45 during 559 

conditioning, assumed to support fear learning. 560

Locus coeruleus and action -mode network together su pport implicit learning 633 

Finally, we examined behavioral adaptations over the course of the experiments. 634 

Participants, initially unfamiliar with the task structure, gradually optimized their response 635 

times to reflect stimulus probabilities, indicating impli cit learning. Optimized response times 636 

may arise from preparatory movement initiation, resulting in quicker responses for expected 637 

stimuli, but slower responses for unexpected stimuli. Crucially, individuals with greater MRI -638 

indexed locus coeruleus integri ty, stronger task -related anterior insula activation, and more 639 

pronounced pupil dilation showed greater differentiation in response times between frequent 640 

and infrequent stimuli. This suggests that the locus coeruleus and its cortical targets play a key 641 

ro le in learning and adapting to novel environments, matching previous findings from rodents 642 

demonstrating that noradrenergic activity is causal for task execution and optimization 111 . In 643 

addition, our observations are in line with a recent study in humans relating higher MRI -644 

indexed locus coeruleus integrity to larger practice effects 112 (quicker response times over the 645 

course of one year for multiple cognitive tasks), which may be considered a type of 646 

uninstructed performance optimization similar to the one observed in this study. Our results 647 

moreover support and extend earlier work using explicit cognitive assessments to link 648 

noradrenergic markers to attention 38,40 , learning, and memory 41,43 .649 

Please note that what we refer to as implicit learning in this manuscript is how initially 650 

naïve participants pick up the statistical regularities of a novel (task) environment without 651 

being explicitly instructed 113,114 . Similar concepts have been discussed under the terms 652 

structure learning 115 or statistica l learning 116,117 in other work. We do not make any 653 

assumptions if participants eventually become aware of these regularities 113 . On a neural 654 

level, implicit relative to explicit learning is thought to be linked to partially distinct brain 655

Late -life age differences in implicit learning and locus coeruleus -related measures 663 

Post -mortem research identified the locus coeruleus as one of the starting points of 664 

Alzheimer’s -related tau accumulation 36 , which may dysregulate noradrenergic activity with 665 

increasing age 31,34 and impair late -life cognition 13,33 . Here we found that a latent measure 666

self -initiated processing, externally -driven noradrenergic responses may be less impaired in 669 

aging 38,123,124 . In addition, we found that this noradrenergic latent variable similarly explained 670 

implicit learning across a ge groups, highlighting its behavioral relevance over the adult 671

Limitations 676 

Small subcortical neuromodulatory nuclei are challenging to assess non -invasively in 677 

humans 47,48 . Thus, studies frequently rely on proxy measures, such as pupil size changes that, 678 

however, have been linked to multiple neuromodulators 53,54,68 (also see Figure 4). Adding 679 

additional modalities (EEG, MRI, PET), we find correlational evidence for a robust 680 

involvement of several brainstem –midbrain neuromodulatory systems (noradrenergic, 681 

dopaminergic, serotonergic) that are known to co -activate and interact 3,125 . Future work, 682 

optimally at ultra -high field strength and higher spatial resolution, is required to better 683 

characterize their crosstalk, potential sex differences therein 126 , a nd how this regulates 684 

behavior. 685
